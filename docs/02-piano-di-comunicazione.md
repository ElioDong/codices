## 1. Summary

This document establishes how the **Codices** project communicates with its
audiences: whom it addresses, what it says, in what tone, through which channels
and with what measurable results.

The plan rests on an observation worth stating at the outset. An individual
eight-week project has no press office and no promotional budget. Its
communication **is the site itself**: there is no gap between what the project
says it is and what the project shows. Consequently the plan is not concerned
with promoting a finished product from the outside, but with designing the
experience of those who arrive at the site without knowing what they will find.

The second observation is that the audiences of Codices have needs that are
incompatible with one another. The lecturer looks for proof of a method; the
student looks for a model to imitate; the scholar looks for data to cite; the
casual visitor tries to understand where they have ended up. A site that
addressed only one of them would fail the other three. The plan resolves the
conflict not with a single compromise but with a **stratification**: the same
information is available at different depths, and each audience stops at the
level that is enough for it.

## 2. Communication objectives

### 2.1 General objective

To make immediately understandable what Codices is, whom it serves and on which
technical choices it rests, for four audiences with different needs and without
shared specialist knowledge.

### 2.2 Specific objectives

| No. | Objective | Indicator | Expected result |
|----|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| C1 | Those who arrive at the site understand what it is about within ten seconds | Reading time of the home page header alone | The title and subtitle suffice to answer "what is it" and "what is it for" |
| C2 | Each audience reaches its own level of depth in no more than two clicks from the home page | Navigation depth | Every piece of content is two clicks away or less |
| C3 | The lecturer recognises the design choices behind the result | Presence of a method page | The "Method" page justifies every technical choice |
| C4 | The scholar can cite the data without ambiguity | Presence of a ready citation and a licence | Every catalogue record carries a complete citation and the licence |
| C5 | No visitor misreads the nature of the data | Presence of an explicit notice | The home page states that the six records are examples |
| C6 | The project is findable by search engines for the relevant queries | Presence of descriptive metadata | Every page has a distinct title, description and Open Graph metadata |

## 3. Audience analysis

### 3.1 Method

The audiences are described using the *personas* technique: fictitious but
plausible profiles, built from a real need and a concrete context of use.
Describing "scholars" in the abstract produces useless guidance; describing a
person looking for something precise produces design decisions.

### 3.2 The four personas

#### Persona 1 — The lecturer

> **Who they are.** Teaches Humanities Informatics.
> They have seen dozens of student projects. They can tell in a few seconds a
> site built from a template from one built by hand.
>
> **What they look for.** The proof that the student understood *why* they made
> a choice, not only *how*. They care more about a stated and debatable
> justification than about a pleasant and silent result.
>
> **How they arrive.** They receive the URL by email the day before the exam.
> They open it from a computer, probably while preparing the session.
>
> **What makes them doubt.** A site that looks like a ready-made template with
> the texts changed. A site that does not declare its own limits. A site that
> embellishes and does not explain.

**Design consequence.** The "Method" page is the heart of the site for this
audience. Every technical choice there is accompanied by its justification and
its cost. The "What the project does not do" section and the notice about the
nature of the data exist primarily for this person.

#### Persona 2 — The student on the course

> **Who they are.** They are preparing the same project for the same exam. They
> are confused about what a "web resource" is and about how large it should be.
>
> **What they look for.** A concrete example from which to understand the level
> of depth expected. Above all they try to understand how large they need to
> make it.
>
> **How they arrive.** From a search engine or from a fellow student.
>
> **What makes them doubt.** A project that is too ambitious, which makes them
> think they are not up to it. Or too bare, which makes them think they can do
> less than necessary.

**Design consequence.** The "The project in figures" section communicates
explicitly that the scale is small and deliberate. The message "it is not the
quantity, but the quality of the description" is written for this person. The
texts explain the choices in a language accessible to anyone who has taken the
course.

#### Persona 3 — The manuscript scholar

> **Who they are.** A researcher or doctoral student in palaeography or
> codicology. They look for descriptions of manuscripts for ongoing research.
>
> **What they look for.** Precise data: measurements, quire structure, type of
> script, decoration. And they seek the certainty of being able to cite them.
>
> **How they arrive.** From a search engine, with a specific query such as
> "gothic textualis historiated initial two columns".
>
> **What makes them doubt.** Vague metadata, absence of controlled vocabularies,
> absence of a licence, impossibility of citing.

**Design consequence.** The catalogue record reports the material data in a
dedicated table and the fifteen Dublin Core elements with an indication of the
controlled vocabulary used. At the bottom of the record there is a ready
citation. The licence is declared twice: in the footer and on the project page.

**Critical note.** This audience is the most demanding, and the project
satisfies it only in part: the records are examples. The plan chooses to declare
this openly instead of hiding it, because a deception discovered costs far more
than a declared limit.

#### Persona 4 — The casual visitor

> **Who they are.** Anyone who arrives at the site without a precise goal: an
> acquaintance who has been shown the link, a curious person, someone who has
> seen a share.
>
> **What they look for.** Initially, nothing. They decide in a few seconds
> whether to stay.
>
> **How they arrive.** From a shared link.
>
> **What makes them doubt.** A page that begins with technical jargon.
> An interface where it is unclear where it leads.

**Design consequence.** The home page opens with a sentence in plain language,
not with a definition of Dublin Core. The illustrations provide an immediate
visual foothold. The navigation has four items, not ten.

### 3.3 Audience–need–response matrix

| Audience | Primary need | Where it is satisfied | Depth |
|----------|-----------------|------------------|:----------:|
| Lecturer | Understand the reasons for the choices | "Method" page | 1 click |
| Student | Gauge the breadth of their own project | Home page, "The project in figures" | 0 clicks |
| Scholar | Read and cite precise metadata | Catalogue record | 2 clicks |
| Casual visitor | Understand what they are looking at | Home page, title and subtitle | 0 clicks |

## 4. Key messages

The messages are phrased as sentences that the audience should be able to repeat
after leaving the site. There are three, in order of priority.

### Message 1 — "Little, but well described"

Six manuscripts are not a collection. They are a demonstration of method. The
project claims its small scale as a choice, not suffers it as a limit.

> *Where it lives:* home page title, "The project in figures" section.
> *What proves it:* the six complete records and the explicit declaration of the
> scope in the project plan.

### Message 2 — "The data survive the site"

A website has a short life. Open standards do not. Codices describes its
manuscripts with Dublin Core and TEI so that the data remain readable even when
the site no longer exists.

> *Where it lives:* "Standards adopted" section on the home page, "Method" page.
> *What proves it:* the section on long-term preservation.

### Message 3 — "Accessible is not an extra feature"

The site is built to be used with a keyboard, with a screen reader, on a
320-pixel screen and in dark theme, without any of this being a patch-on
addition.

> *Where it lives:* "Method" page, "Accessibility" section.
> *What proves it:* the WCAG requirements table with the way in which
> each one is satisfied.

### 4.1 What the plan chooses not to say

A communication plan is also a list of what is left unsaid. Codices does not
communicate:

- **Novelty or current relevance.** There is nothing new: the manuscripts are a
  thousand years old.
- **Urgency.** No action is required of the visitor.
- **Superiority over other projects.** The tone is not competitive.
- **Richness of the collection.** It would be false, and audience 3 would notice.

## 5. Identity and tone of voice

### 5.1 Personality

Codices speaks like a **well-made catalogue**: precise, sober, competent, never
emphatic. It is not a commercial product and does not pretend to be one. The
reference model is not the site of a technology company but the page of a
university library written by someone who loves their work.

### 5.2 The four attributes of the tone

| Attribute | It means | It does not mean |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Precise** | Using the correct technical term when needed | Using jargon to exclude those who do not know it |
| **Sober** | No superfluous adjective, no exclamation mark | Being dry or bureaucratic |
| **Transparent** | Declaring limits before the audience discovers them | Flagellating oneself or belittling the work |
| **Cultured** | Taking the reader's intellectual curiosity for granted | Taking specialist knowledge for granted |

### 5.3 Writing rules

1. **The technical term is used, but explained the first time it appears.**
   "Dublin Core" on first use is followed by a sentence saying what it is.
2. **No exclamation marks.** No exception.
3. **No "click here".** The link text describes the destination.
4. **The voice is active.** "The project adopts Dublin Core", not "Dublin Core
   was adopted by the project".
5. **Numbers are numbers.** "Six manuscripts", not "several manuscripts".
6. **Commas are not used to soften.** If a sentence needs many commas, it should
   be rewritten.
7. **Limits are declared explicitly.** The "What the project does not do" section
   is written in this register.

### 5.4 Visual system

The visual identity is consistent with the tone: sober, warm, inspired by the
materials of the manuscript book but without citationism.

| Element | Choice | Justification |
|----------|--------|-------------|
| Primary colour | Red lead `#8c2f39` | It is the colour of rubrics, the parts written in red in codices |
| Secondary colour | Gold leaf `#9a7620` | It echoes the gilding of illuminated initials |
| Light background | Parchment `#fbf7ef` | It evokes the support without imitating its texture |
| Dark background | Brown `#14110d` | A warm black, not an absolute black |
| Heading typeface | Iowan Old Style, Palatino, Georgia | A serif family, suited to long reading |
| Body typeface | Inter, system typeface | High on-screen legibility, no network loading |
| Illustrations | Vector, original, abstract | They avoid the copyright problem and weigh only a few kilobytes |

The visual system is expressed entirely in **CSS custom properties**, so that a
change to a single value propagates across the whole site. This choice is also a
communicative statement: the project is built to be modified by whoever comes
after.

## 6. Channels

### 6.1 The selection principle

Channels are chosen on the basis of a single question: **where is the audience
already?** A channel is not opened for the sake of having it.

### 6.2 Channels activated

| Channel | Main audience | Content | Cost | Frequency |
|--------|--------------------|-----------|------:|-----------|
| **The site itself** | All | Complete content, five pages | €0 | Permanent |
| **Search engines** | Scholar, student | Descriptive metadata, distinct titles per page | €0 | Permanent |
| **Link sharing** | Casual visitor | Open Graph metadata for the preview | €0 | Once |
| **Communication to the lecturer** | Lecturer | Message with URL, one day before | €0 | Once |
| **Oral presentation at the exam** | Lecturer | Discussion of the design choices | €0 | Once |

### 6.3 Channels deliberately not activated

| Channel | Reason for exclusion |
|--------|-----------------------|
| Social networks | The audience is not there; the management cost outweighs the benefit |
| Newsletter | No recurring audience to retain over eight weeks |
| Blog | It would duplicate the "Method" page without adding anything |
| Paid advertising | No budget and no audience to reach en masse |
| Press release | The project has no news value |

This table has a precise communicative value: it shows that the absence of these
channels is a decision and not an oversight.

### 6.4 The site as a stratified channel

The stratification of the content is the central communicative choice of the
project. The same information is available at three depths:

| Level | Where | Reading time | For whom |
|---------|------|-----------------:|---------|
| **Summary** | Title and subtitle of the home page | 10 s | Casual visitor |
| **Orientation** | Home page sections, figures, flow steps | 2 min | Student |
| **In-depth** | "Method" page, catalogue record | 10 min | Lecturer, scholar |

No level necessarily leads to the next. Those who stop at the first have
understood what Codices is; those who reach the third have understood how it was
built.

## 7. Editorial calendar

The project **has no editorial cadence**: the content is published once and not
updated. The calendar that follows is therefore a production and communication
calendar, not one of periodic publication.

| Week | Communication activity | Content produced | Responsible |
|:---------:|---------------------------|--------------------|--------------|
| 1 | Definition of the key messages and the audiences | This plan, first draft | Designer |
| 2 | Definition of the tone of voice and the writing rules | Writing rules, visual system | Editor |
| 3 | Testing the visual hierarchy on a prototype | Home page and record in draft | Developer |
| 4 | Verification of comprehensibility with an external reader | Corrections to the home page texts | Editor |
| 5 | Drafting the texts of the records and of the method | Final texts | Editor |
| 6 | Preparation of the metadata for search engines | Titles, descriptions, Open Graph | Developer |
| 7 | Final review of the texts and the links | List of corrections | Verifier |
| 8 | **Going live and communication of the URL to the lecturer** | Site published and message sent | Project manager |

### 7.1 The critical moment

The communication to the lecturer is not a bureaucratic formality: it is the
moment when the project enters its only moment of assessment. The deadline —
**12:00 on the day before the exam** — is a rigid constraint of the course, and
failing to meet it entails exclusion from the exam.

For this reason the plan provides for an **advance rule**: the URL is
communicated at the moment of going live, in week 8, and not on the day of the
deadline. The message contains three elements: the address, a sentence saying
what one is looking at and the list of the two documents submitted.

## 8. Contingency plans

### 8.1 What to do if the site is unreachable on the day of the exam

| Scenario | Immediate action | Prevention |
|----------|------------------|-------------|
| The hosting service is down | Activate a copy on a second service already prepared | Keep a mirror copy ready from the start |
| The free domain has expired | Renew and communicate any new address | Check the expiry a week in advance |
| The site opens but a resource is missing | Check the relative paths of the files | Use relative paths, never absolute |
| The lecturer cannot find the message | Bring a copy of the site on a physical medium | Keep a working local copy |

The general rule is that the project must be able to be shown **even without a
network**. The site is static by construction: a copy of the files on a USB stick
works exactly like the online version.

### 8.2 What to do if a text turns out to be incomprehensible

During week 4 the plan provides for a comprehensibility test with a reader
external to the project. If a section is not understood:

1. The exact sentence that created the misunderstanding is identified.
2. That sentence is rewritten, not the entire section.
3. The test is repeated on the modified sentence alone.

The comprehensibility test was not extended to audiences 3 and 4 for lack of
time. It is a declared limit of this plan.

## 9. Measurement

### 9.1 Indicators

For a project without statistical analysis tools, the indicators must be
verifiable without collecting data on users. The plan deliberately refuses the
installation of tracking tools: they are not necessary to the objectives and
they raise a privacy problem disproportionate to the scale of the project.

| Indicator | How it is measured | Threshold | Outcome |
|-----------|----------------|--------|-------|
| Immediate comprehensibility | Test with five external readers: can they say what the site is after ten seconds? | 5 out of 5 | 4 out of 5 |
| Navigation depth | Number of clicks needed to reach each piece of content from the home page | ≤ 2 | 2 |
| Completeness of citability | Every record has a ready citation and licence | 6 out of 6 | 6 out of 6 |
| Clarity about limits | The home page declares the example nature of the data | Yes | Yes |
| Validity of metadata | Every page has a distinct title, description and Open Graph | 5 out of 5 | 5 out of 5 |
| Accessibility of the texts | The tone respects the seven writing rules | No violation | No violation |

### 9.2 What went wrong

**The comprehensibility test gave 4 out of 5.** One reader out of five, after
reading the title and subtitle of the home page, concluded that the site was the
real catalogue of an existing library and not a prototype with example data.

The cause was identified precisely: the title — "Six illuminated manuscripts,
described once and for all, readable by anyone" — is effective at the level of
the message but contains no element signalling its nature as a prototype. The
notice exists, but it is placed at the bottom of the page, where a reader who
stops at the first ten seconds does not reach it.

**Correction applied.** The kicker above the title, which previously read
"Project for computer methods for the humanities", was modified to declare the
nature of the site straight away. The test repeated with two new readers gave a
positive result.

**Lesson learned.** A correct notice placed at the bottom does not protect
against misunderstanding: those who stop earlier do not read it. If a piece of
information is necessary to avoid a misunderstanding, it must be on the first
screen, even at the cost of weakening the incisiveness of the title.

### 9.3 What was not measured

The plan explicitly declares what it could not verify:

- **The behaviour of real users over time.** There was no way to observe how the
  site is used beyond the single test session.
- **Performance with real assistive technologies.** The accessibility check was
  conducted with an automated tool and with keyboard navigation alone, not with
  a screen reader and not with people with disabilities.
- **Actual findability on search engines.** The metadata are present, but
  indexing takes time and was not observed.
